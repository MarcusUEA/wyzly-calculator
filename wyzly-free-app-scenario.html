The evidence behind the guide ranges

  • Apps for parents and children that went free first. Life360 was free-only from 2008 to 2013 and had 40M+ registered users when it added a paid tier; paying circles have sat at about 3% of monthly actives for eight years, 77% of new users arrived organically, and it now has 102M monthly actives (2019 prospectus, Q2 2026). ClassDojo was free for eight years, reached 51M users with no paid acquisition, then added a $7.99 parent tier and was profitable within four months with "hundreds of thousands" paying (TechCrunch, 2021). OurPact, a parental-control app, ran completely free to 600,000 users before charging (San Diego Business Journal). Lingokids went the other way in 2023, paid to free, and monthly actives rose 350% in a year (Kidscreen).
  • Install → user in parenting apps. Family Insights, a listed parental-control app, took parent-app setup completion from 28% to 95% with onboarding work, while first-week child-device pairing moved only from 3% to 19% (Stockhead, 2019). That is why a user here is an account created, with the child link as the next step, and why the guide runs 30% to 60%. Microsoft's free Family Safety app shows the other trap: 5M+ installs, roughly 140k active (Sensor Tower) — a download is not a user.
  • What share of a parent base pays, for later. Kinedu: 3.3M registered, 500k monthly actives, 30k paying (MIT Solve, 2019). Epic!: 1.7M paying subscribers against 10M children reached (Publishers Weekly, 2019). Qustodio runs freemium at $21M ARR on millions of users (Qoria, 2025). Noggin, paid-only, shut down in 2024.
  • Other free sign-up rates, for the wider picture. Finance apps with identity checks 21% (Liftoff, 2022); e-commerce 32% in week one (CleverTap, 2023); dating, where signing up is the product, ~80% on iOS (Liftoff, 2022).
  • Click → install. Facebook 13.1%, Instagram 15.1% across 10M installs (Branch, 2017). No published evidence that removing a price lifts this step, so 0% is a fair pick and +30% is the top of the guide.
  • The audience. 47% of US parents use parental controls on smartphones (FOSI/Ipsos, 2025); 86% of parents of under-13s have screen-time rules (Pew, 2025). No benchmark vendor publishes a parenting-category funnel; everything above is company-disclosed or filed.
  • Today's Wyzly funnel. Cost per click $0.86–1.31, click → install 6–8%, install → subscriber 12–19% (Meta 18.6%), subscriber → paid 45% today.

The full table of ~80 sourced figures is on the Evidence tab of the accompanying workbook; section A is apps for parents and children.

What about word of mouth and critical mass?secondary · every user brings others, and a free app is easier to recommend

The money section counts only the users the media buys. Every user also brings others — word of mouth, refer-a-friend, app-store search — and a free app has two advantages: a far bigger base, and nothing for the person you recommend it to to pay. One number captures it for each route: the K-factor, the extra users each user brings over their lifetime. Users ÷ (1 − K) = users including word of mouth.

Critical mass · the first month each route passes a threshold

Users including word of mouth, with referrals spread evenly over the period — in reality they lag the base by months, so read these as earliest months. The seed plan's own note: below about 200 paying families the referral loop is too small to measure; above it every family becomes a channel. Opal took three and a half years to go from 200k to 10M users on freemium; Life360 was free for five years before it charged.

A K-factor model is the standard back-of-envelope for virality and no more than that: it assumes every user refers at the same rate forever, with no saturation and no timing. It shows the size of the lever and what it would take, not a forecast.
